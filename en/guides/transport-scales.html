Fluid mechanics: theory and checks

Thermal and mass diffusion: the distinct roles of Pr, Sc, Pe and Nu

Derive diffusion scales, compare them with transport times, and identify what a heat-transfer coefficient actually supplies.

One Reynolds number does not determine every field

Momentum, heat and a dilute solute diffuse at different rates in the same velocity field. Assume a Newtonian fluid, constant isotropic properties, no reaction and no heat source. Strongly temperature- or concentration-dependent properties need a coupled model.

First compare diffusion over a shared exposure time, then separate that estimate from a convective boundary layer. α and D share units of m²/s but are different inputs. The MIT 3.185 notes state that Schmidt numbers in liquids are uniformly much greater than one, while heat-transfer Prandtl numbers are smaller and fall well below one for liquid metals.

Obtaining a diffusion scale

Scaling ∂T/∂t = α∂²T/∂y² in a stationary medium as ΔT/t ≈ αΔT/δ² gives δ ≈ √(αt); likewise δν ≈ √(νt) and δD ≈ √(Dt). These are order-of-magnitude lengths, not a 99% thickness; coefficients depend on boundary conditions and the thickness definition.

Pr = ν/α; Sc = ν/D
δν/δα ≈ √Pr; δν/δD ≈ √Sc

The square-root ratios belong to this shared-time comparison, not to convective boundary layers, where near-wall speed varies and couples to the scalar field. For a laminar flat-plate layer the MIT 3.185 notes give δu/δT = 0.975 Pr1/3 for Pr above about 0.6; the exponent 1/3 reflects a nearly linear velocity profile inside a thin thermal layer. Example 2 compares the two.

An advection-time cross-check

Example 1. One velocity, three diffusion lengths

Given (round numbers, not a measured fluid): ν = 10⁻⁶, α = 2×10⁻⁷ and D = 10⁻⁹ m²/s, v = 0.2 m/s, L = 0.05 m. Calculator inputs ρ = 1000, μ = 0.001, k = 0.6 and cₚ = 3000 reproduce this ν and α.

tadv = L/v = 0.25 s

Diffusion scales over tadv: δν = 0.500 mm, δα = 0.2236 mm, δD = 0.01581 mm

Re = vL/ν = 10000, Pr = 5, Sc = 1000

Thermal Pe = vL/α = 50000 = Re × Pr

Species Pe = vL/D = 10000000 = Re × Sc

Check: δ/L ≈ 1/√Pe, and 1/√50000 × 50 mm = 0.2236 mm. Large Pe means advection crosses L faster than diffusion does; diffusion can still control transfer over the short wall-normal distance where the 0.016 mm solute layer sits.

Example 2. Oxygen absorbed into still water for one second

Given: 20 °C properties from the table and an assumed exposure time t = 1 s, such as a brief contact between a bubble and the liquid.

α = k/(ρcₚ) = 0.5995/(998.2 × 4185) = 1.4351 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s

Pr = ν/α = 6.982 (the table lists 6.981; the difference is rounding in k and cₚ)

Sc = ν/D = 1.0020 × 10⁻⁶ / 1.97 × 10⁻⁹ = 508.6

After 1 s: δν = 1.001 mm, δα = 0.3788 mm, δD = 0.0444 mm

Shared-time ratios: √Pr = 2.642, √Sc = 22.55; laminar flat-plate form 0.975 Pr1/3 = 1.863

At 60 °C: Sc = 4.757 × 10⁻⁷ / 4.82 × 10⁻⁹ = 98.69

Oxygen penetrates about 44 µm in a second, one twenty-third of the momentum length, so resolving the liquid-side concentration layer in CFD needs much finer near-interface cells than the velocity field. From 20 °C to 60 °C, ν falls and D rises, lowering Sc about 5.2-fold; a Sc taken at the wrong temperature misjudges √Sc by more than a factor of two. The gap between 2.642 and 1.863 shows that the shared-time ratio is not a boundary-layer result.

Nusselt number requires a heat-transfer coefficient

Wall heat flux q″ = h(Twall − Treference) equals Fourier conduction −k(∂T/∂n)wall, so h carries the wall gradient. Only for a linear layer of effective thickness δeff is h = k/δeff and Nu = hL/k = L/δeff; δeff is neither the diffusion estimate nor a 99% thickness.

Example 3. From a supplied h to Nu, and from a known Nu back to h

Part A. With a supplied h = 120 W/(m²·K), k = 0.6 W/(m·K) and L = 0.05 m, Nu = 10; a 20 K difference gives q″ = 2400 W/m². The 120 is an input, not a prediction from Pe.

Part B. Water at 20 °C flows at an assumed 0.1 m/s through a smooth tube of assumed d = 10 mm, far from the inlet, with uniform wall temperature. For fully developed laminar tube flow the INL RELAP-7 manual uses Nu = 3.66 (constant wall temperature) and 4.36 (constant heat flux).

Re = ρvd/μ = 998.2 × 0.1 × 0.01 / 1.0000 × 10⁻³ = 998.2 (laminar)

Pe = Re × Pr = 6968

h = Nu k/d = 3.66 × 0.5995 / 0.01 = 219.4 W/(m²·K); with constant heat flux, 261.4 W/(m²·K)

Effective conduction length δeff = d/Nu = 2.732 mm

Commonly quoted thermal entry estimate 0.05 Re Pr d ≈ 3.48 m

Nu = 3.66 holds only after the temperature profile develops, which here takes metres of tube. A 1 m heated section lies mostly in the entry region, where local Nu exceeds 3.66. The wall condition alone changes h by 19%, so state it.

Predicting h needs a solution or correlation with stated geometry, regime, local or average convention, wall condition and validity range.

Conditions to keep in a comparison

  • Use the same length, speed and property conditions for Re and Pe checks.
  • Use α for heat and the appropriate species D for mass transfer, evaluated at the same temperature as ν.
  • Distinguish diffusion estimates from boundary-layer thickness definitions.
  • Record the reference temperature and whether h is local or averaged.
  • Matching Pr or Sc alone does not match geometry, wall conditions or transfer rates.
  • Check that a fully developed Nu applies at the stations you evaluate, using an entry-length estimate.

Questions about diffusion scales and Nu

Why is √(αt) not the thermal boundary-layer thickness?

It is a stationary-medium scale with no boundary condition or thickness definition. A convective layer depends on the velocity profile, distance from the leading edge and wall condition. Use √(αt) for orders of magnitude and a boundary-layer solution or correlation for a thickness or h.

At what temperature should I evaluate Sc?

Evaluate ν and D at the same temperature, usually the film or bulk temperature your correlation specifies. In Example 2, Sc falls from 508.6 at 20 °C to 98.69 at 60 °C; mixing the two states describes neither.

Can I estimate h from the Peclet number alone?

No. Pe compares advection with diffusion over a length; h also depends on geometry, regime, wall condition and development length. In Example 3 the wall condition alone gives 219.4 or 261.4 W/(m²·K).

Why does my computed Pr differ slightly from the property table?

Tables round k, cₚ and μ independently: for IAEA 20 °C water, μcₚ/k gives 6.981 and ν/α gives 6.982. For identity checks such as Pe = Re × Pr, compute Pr from the same μ, cₚ and k used elsewhere.
