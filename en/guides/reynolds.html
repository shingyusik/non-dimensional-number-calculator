Method and interpretation

Reynolds Number

See how to enter values, calculate a result, and check the limits of its interpretation.

Inertial stress over viscous stress

Re compares the stress created by inertia with the stress created by viscosity. A velocity change v produces a pressure change of order ρv², and a velocity gradient over L produces a shear stress of order μv/L; their ratio is Re = ρvL/μ. The MIT HST notes define it with tube diameter d as Red = ρvd/μ = vd/ν, and NASA Glenn derives the same form from the inertial and viscous terms of the momentum equation.

At high Re, viscosity matters only in thin boundary layers and wakes; at low Re it governs the whole flow. NASA Glenn puts it as Re near 10 million (viscous forces small, flow essentially inviscid) versus near 100 (viscosity must be considered). Drela's MIT notes add that separated bluff-body flows stay viscosity-dominated at any Re; chord Re runs from about 5,000 (butterfly) to 50 million (Boeing 777).

Dynamic versus kinematic viscosity, and the length scale

Re = ρvL / μ = vL / ν

ρ is density (kg/m³), v speed (m/s), L the characteristic length (m) and μ dynamic viscosity (Pa·s). With kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ (m²/s), Re = vL/ν needs no density. Entering ν where μ belongs is off by a factor of ρ. In gases μ barely depends on pressure, but ρ and ν do, so match the pressure as well as the temperature.

Inside a round pipe, use the inner diameter and the mean velocity. For non-circular ducts use the hydraulic diameter Dh = 4A/P (area A, wetted perimeter P). External flows use a conventional length (cylinder diameter, wing chord); check L before comparing.

Examples: household pipe, aorta, HVAC duct

Example 1. Household water supply pipe

Given: inner diameter 15 mm and mean velocity 1.2 m/s (assumed). Water at 20 °C from IAEA Table 3.3: ρ = 998.2 kg/m³, μ = 1.0000×10⁻³ Pa·s, ν = 1.0020×10⁻⁶ m²/s.

Units: D = 15 mm = 0.015 m. Flow rate (π/4) × 0.015² × 1.2 m³/s, about 12.7 L per minute.

Calculation: Re = 998.2 × 1.2 × 0.015 ÷ 0.001 = 17,968. Using ν gives 1.2 × 0.015 ÷ 1.0020×10⁻⁶ = 17,964; the 0.02% gap is rounding between μ/ρ = 1.0018×10⁻⁶ and the tabulated ν.

Interpretation: well above 2300, so the flow at normal tap rates is turbulent. Re = 2300 would need 2300 × 0.001 ÷ (998.2 × 0.015) = 0.154 m/s, about 1.63 L per minute. With 60 °C water (IAEA Table 3.3, ρ = 983.2 kg/m³, μ = 4.677×10⁻⁴ Pa·s) at the same speed, Re = 37,840, about 2.1 times the cold-water value.

Example 2. Mean Reynolds number in the human aorta

Given: mean velocity 48 cm/s and diameter 2.5 cm for the human aorta (MIT HST, Table 1) and whole-blood viscosity of about 4 cP from the same notes. Blood density 1050 kg/m³ is an assumption.

Units: v = 0.48 m/s, D = 0.025 m, μ = 4 cP = 0.004 Pa·s.

Calculation: Re = 1050 × 0.48 × 0.025 ÷ 0.004 = 3,150. A density of 1000 or 1100 kg/m³ gives 3,000 to 3,300, so the assumption moves the result by about ±5%.

Interpretation: the calculator says transitional, but do not take that literally. Blood is non-Newtonian (4 cP is a large-vessel approximation) and aortic flow is pulsatile, so a mean-velocity Re misses instantaneous conditions. The MIT notes report that flow through the normal aortic valve is observed to be laminar despite peak Re near 10⁴, which a steady-pipe threshold would not predict. The notes' Table 5 lists a peak Re of 4500 in the ascending aorta, but that is dog data.

Example 3. Rectangular HVAC duct with a hydraulic diameter

Given: a 400 mm × 200 mm section and 4 m/s mean air speed (assumed). Air at 20 °C, ν = 1.5104×10⁻⁵ m²/s at 1 atm (from IAEA Table 3.1 μ and the ideal-gas density).

Units: A = 0.4 × 0.2 = 0.08 m², P = 2 × (0.4 + 0.2) = 1.2 m, so Dh = 4 × 0.08 ÷ 1.2 = 0.267 m.

Calculation: with the unrounded Dh (0.26666… m), Re = 4 × Dh ÷ 1.5104×10⁻⁵ = 70,621. The 98.1 kPa table value ν = 15.61×10⁻⁶ gives 68,332, about 3% lower from pressure alone.

Interpretation: using the short side 0.2 m gives 52,966 and the long side 0.4 m gives 105,932, a factor of two apart. Duct friction correlations use Dh. The flow is turbulent either way, but round-pipe thresholds such as 2300 need not hold for a rectangle.

Reading 2300 and 4000 as boundaries

The MIT HST notes say flow in a uniform circular tube "typically becomes turbulent when Red > 2300", and that flow leaving a narrow orifice into a larger tube or reservoir can turn turbulent at Re as low as 10. So 2300 is a typical value for fully developed flow in a smooth round pipe; inlet disturbances, vibration, roughness, bends and sudden expansions all move it.

Documents also differ. The TU Delft text on LibreTexts uses Re < 2320 for the laminar 64/Re range, and the U.S. DOE WIPP performance assessment assumes a single 2100 cutoff in a drilling-fluid model. This site's calculator shows Re < 2300 as laminar, Re > 4000 as turbulent and the rest as transitional; the comparisons are strict, so exactly 2300 or 4000 is labelled transitional.

For laminar flow, the MIT HST entrance-length estimate Le/D = 0.065 Red gives 130 diameters at Re = 2000. In shorter pipes, fully developed friction and heat-transfer correlations do not apply.

Common mistakes when computing Re

  • Units: skipping mm to m or cP to Pa·s (1 cP = 0.001 Pa·s) makes Re wrong by 1000; cm/s must be divided by 100.
  • Velocity: use flow rate over area. In laminar flow the centre-line maximum is twice the mean, which doubles Re.
  • Property temperature: water viscosity changes by more than a factor of two between 20 °C and 60 °C.
  • Stopping at Re: Re does not give pressure loss or heat transfer; those also need pipe length and a friction factor, or Pr and a Nusselt correlation.

Frequently asked questions about Reynolds number calculations

What happens if I put kinematic viscosity ν in the μ field?

The result is multiplied by ρ: about 1000 times too large for water and about 1.2 times for air. Convert with μ = ρν or compute Re = vL/ν directly.

Some sources use 2000 or 2100 instead of 2300. Which is right?

All are conventions for round-pipe transition, which really depends on inlet conditions and disturbances. Near the boundary, compute pressure loss or heat transfer for both regimes and check the design margin.

The calculator shows Re = 4000 as transitional. Is that a bug?

No. It labels turbulent only above 4000, so exactly 4000 falls in the transitional band. Real flow has no sharp line between 4000 and 4001.

Can Re be computed for fluids like blood whose viscosity varies?

Yes, with a representative viscosity, but only approximately. MIT HST describes whole blood as shear-thinning; about 4 cP is common for large vessels, while small vessels and low shear rates give different apparent viscosities.

Which length goes into L for a rectangular duct?

The hydraulic diameter Dh = 4A/P, 0.267 m for the 400 mm × 200 mm duct in Example 3. One side length gives an Re that does not match friction correlations.

Related dimensionless numbers

Record Re with these, on the same length and speed.

  • Froude number (Fr): free-surface model tests that match Fr miss Re, so compare both and decide which to give up.
  • Weber number (We): with the same L and v, We/Re = μv/σ shows whether viscosity or surface tension leads in drops and sprays.
  • Strouhal number (St): cylinder shedding St changes with Re range, so record Re with it.
  • Péclet number (Pe): Re times Pr gives the thermal Pe, showing whether momentum or heat diffuses faster.
