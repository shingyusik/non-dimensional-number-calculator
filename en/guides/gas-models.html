Fluid mechanics: theory and checks

Choosing gas-flow models: check Mach and Knudsen numbers separately

Compressibility and molecular length scales test different assumptions. Use isentropic relations to quantify a comparison.

Two independent modeling questions

Incompressibility asks whether density variation is negligible; the continuum assumption asks whether molecular travel distances are small against the relevant length. Low Ma does not establish continuum behavior, and small Kn does not establish constant density.

The acoustic and stagnation relations below assume a calorically perfect ideal gas with constant γ (NASA Glenn gives γ = 1.4 for air at standard day conditions) and adiabatic, isentropic deceleration. They do not cover shocks, strong friction, heat input or rarefaction.

Deriving the stagnation-state comparison

For this ideal gas c² = γRT and Ma = v/c. Reversible adiabatic deceleration conserves energy: cₚT₀ = cₚT + v²/2. Inserting cₚ = γR/(γ−1) gives the first ratio below; p/ρᵞ = constant with the ideal-gas equation gives the other two.

T₀/T = 1 + (γ−1)Ma²/2
ρ₀/ρ = (T₀/T)^(1/(γ−1))
p₀/p = (T₀/T)^(γ/(γ−1))
(p₀ − p)/(½ρv²) = (p₀/p − 1)/(γMa²/2)

The last line uses ½ρv² = γpMa²/2. It shows how far the isentropic pressure rise exceeds the Bernoulli value ½ρv², often the more practical error for a pitot reading or pressure coefficient. These ratios compare a local state with ideal deceleration, not inlet-to-outlet changes in a real duct.

Stagnation ratios at fixed temperature

Example 1. Ma = 0.3 and 0.8 at T = 300 K

Given: γ = 1.4, R = 287 J/(kg·K) and an assumed static temperature T = 300 K, so c = √120540 = 347.1887 m/s. The speeds are chosen to give Ma = 0.3 and 0.8; they are not atmospheric data.

At Ma = 0.3 the density ratio is about 4.56% above unity, yet the isentropic pressure rise exceeds ½ρv² by only about 2.3%. The answer to "is Ma = 0.3 incompressible?" therefore depends on whether density or pressure accuracy matters for your result.

Using standard-atmosphere data

Example 2. Ma = 0.8 at 11 000 m

Given: the standard atmosphere at 11 000 m from Table 1.1 of the Cornell MAE 5070 notes: T = 216.65 K, p = 22631.70 Pa, ρ = 0.363912 kg/m³, a = 295.07 m/s. The flight Mach number 0.8 is assumed.

Check: √(1.4 × 287 × 216.65) = 295.04 m/s; back-calculating R from the table row gives 287.05 J/(kg·K)

v = 0.8 × 295.07 = 236.06 m/s

T₀ = 216.65 × 1.128 = 244.38 K

p₀ = 22631.70 × 1.52434 = 34498 Pa; ρ₀ = 0.363912 × 1.35137 = 0.4918 kg/m³

½ρv² = 10139 Pa; isentropic p₀ − p = 11867 Pa

An incompressible pitot reduction would read 11867 Pa as dynamic pressure and overestimate v² by about 17%; the ratio 11867/10139 is the same 1.170 as Example 1 because it depends only on Ma and γ. The 0.01% gap between 295.04 and 295.07 m/s comes from rounding R to 287; it matters only when comparing sound speeds to five digits.

Check molecular scales after changing device size or pressure

Hold λ = 6.8×10⁻⁸ m fixed (NIST's 67.3 nm, rounded). At L = 0.001 m, Kn = 6.8×10⁻⁵; at L = 10⁻⁶ m, Kn = 0.068. Ma is unchanged at fixed speed, so size affects a separate assumption. At Kn = 0.068, assess wall slip and temperature jump before reusing a no-slip model.

Example 3. A 10 µm channel as the pressure drops

Given: λ = 67.3 nm in air at 23 °C and 101.3 kPa (NIST). Channel width L = 10 µm and the pressures 10 kPa and 1 kPa are assumed. At fixed temperature, kinetic theory gives λ ∝ 1/p, so λ is scaled by 101.3/p.

101.3 kPa: Kn = 67.3 nm / 10 µm = 0.00673 (continuum)

10 kPa: λ = 0.6817 µm, Kn = 0.0682 (slip)

1 kPa: λ = 6.817 µm, Kn = 0.682 (transition)

Ma is unchanged by these pressure steps when T and v stay fixed, because c = √(γRT) does not depend on pressure. A low-speed vacuum line can therefore pass every compressibility check and still need a slip or kinetic model.

What to record when selecting a model

  • State local temperature and the acoustic assumptions behind Ma.
  • Choose an acceptable density or pressure error for an incompressible approximation.
  • Explain which gas state and physical length λ and L represent.
  • Do not apply isentropic ratios across a shock.
  • Strong rarefaction or thermal nonequilibrium requires more than this ideal stagnation comparison.

Questions about Mach, Knudsen and model choice

Is Ma < 0.3 enough to treat a gas flow as incompressible?

Only if the error is acceptable: at Ma = 0.3 stagnation density is 4.56% above static density and the pressure rise is 2.3% above ½ρv².

Why does √(γRT) give 295.04 m/s at 11 000 m when the table lists 295.07?

The table row corresponds to R ≈ 287.05 J/(kg·K); using 287 lowers c by about 0.01%. Use the same R as your solver when comparing digits.

Does lowering the pressure change Ma or Kn?

At fixed temperature and speed, Ma stays the same because c depends on T, while λ grows as 1/p. In Example 3 a drop from 101.3 kPa to 1 kPa moves Kn from 0.00673 to 0.682.

Why do aerosol papers report a different Kn for the same particle?

The NIST slip-correction paper defines Kn = 2λ/Dp, using the particle radius rather than its diameter as the length. Check the definition before applying a regime boundary.

Can I use isentropic relations across a shock?

No. NASA Glenn notes that abrupt compression creates shocks, the flow becomes irreversible and the oblique or normal shock relations apply instead.
