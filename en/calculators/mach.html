Why divide by the speed of sound?

How much a gas changes density as it speeds up depends on its speed relative to the speed of sound, not on the absolute speed. The same 200 m/s gives different Mach numbers in cold and warm air. The Mach number is the first check on whether compressibility matters.

Treating air as an ideal gas with γ = 1.4 and R = 287 J/(kg·K), the speed of sound is c = √(γRT). At 300 K that is √(1.4 × 287 × 300) ≈ 347 m/s. T must be an absolute temperature in kelvin.

Choosing the speed of sound

  • The 340 m/s placeholder is a rough room-temperature value for air. Recalculate c when temperature or altitude changes.
  • Where temperature varies along the flow, as in a nozzle, use the local speed of sound at the point of interest.
  • Convert km/h to m/s by dividing by 3.6. One knot is about 0.514 m/s.
  • Gases other than air have different γ and R, so do not reuse the value for air.

What the regime labels mean

The calculator labels Ma < 0.8 subsonic, 0.8 to 1.2 transonic, 1.2 to 5 supersonic and 5 or more hypersonic. These are conventional, approximate limits that vary between references. In the transonic band the free stream is still below the speed of sound, yet the flow can locally exceed it where it accelerates, such as over the upper surface of a wing.

Ma ≈ 0.3 is a common reference when judging whether the incompressible approximation is acceptable. From the isentropic relation with γ = 1.4, the ratio of stagnation to static density at Ma = 0.3 is about 1.046. Whether that difference is acceptable depends on the accuracy you need.

Check with the example

The example uses v = 170 m/s and c = 340 m/s, so Ma = 0.5. The same 170 m/s in colder air with c = 300 m/s gives about 0.567. Remembering that the Mach number can rise while the speed stays the same avoids mistakes when comparing altitudes.

What the Mach number cannot tell you

Ma alone does not locate shock waves or give pressure distributions, drag or structural margins. Check viscous effects with the Reynolds number and molecular length scales with the Knudsen number. The gas-model note explains why those checks stay separate.
