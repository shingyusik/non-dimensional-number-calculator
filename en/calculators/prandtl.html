Why Pr depends only on the fluid

Pr = μcₚ/k equals ν/α, kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ divided by thermal diffusivity α = k/(ρcₚ). There is no speed or length in it, so the same fluid at the same temperature and pressure has the same Pr whether it flows through a pipe or over a plate. Reynolds and Péclet numbers describe the flow; the Prandtl number describes the fluid.

Common unit mistakes

  • Specific heat in kJ/(kg·K) must be multiplied by 1000. Entering 4.18 instead of 4180 makes Pr a thousand times too small.
  • Viscosity in cP must be multiplied by 0.001 to give Pa·s.
  • Do not put kinematic viscosity ν (m²/s) in the μ field; the result is off by a factor of the density.
  • Taking μ, cₚ and k from tables at different temperatures gives a meaningless result. Liquid viscosity in particular is very temperature-sensitive.

Reading the size of Pr

Pr above 1 means momentum diffuses faster than heat; in laminar boundary layers the velocity layer then tends to be thicker than the thermal layer, and the opposite holds below 1. As rough orders of magnitude, gases sit near 1 (air about 0.7), water at room temperature is in single digits, viscous oils reach hundreds or more, and liquid metals are far below 1. A result far outside these ranges is a cue to recheck the units.

Example and cross-check

The example uses μ = 0.001 Pa·s, cₚ = 4180 J/(kg·K) and k = 0.6 W/(m·K), giving Pr ≈ 6.97. The pipe-flow note uses cₚ = 4200 to get Pr = 7, confirms it again as ν/α, and shows that multiplying by Re = 2000 reproduces the Péclet number of 14000.

Pr alone does not give a heat-transfer coefficient or a heat flow. It is an input to convective correlations together with Re, or a way to compare the boundary-layer behaviour of two fluids.
