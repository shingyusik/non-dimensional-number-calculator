Weber number calculator (We)

Calculate the Weber number from density, speed, a length and surface tension. It compares inertia with surface tension where the shape of an interface matters, as in drops, bubbles and sprays.

A ratio of two pressure scales

We = ρv²L/σ divides the inertial pressure scale ρv² by the surface-tension pressure scale σ/L. The curvature pressure that makes the inside of a curved interface higher than the outside is 2σ/R for radius R; the surface-tension note computes 144 Pa for a water drop of radius 1 mm with σ = 0.072 N/m. A large We means the flow is strong enough to overcome pressures like this and deform the interface.

Points to watch when entering values

  • Speed is squared: with everything else fixed, doubling v quadruples We. Use the speed of the drop relative to the surrounding fluid.
  • Surface tension in mN/m (or dyn/cm) must be divided by 1000 to give N/m; 72 mN/m is 0.072 N/m.
  • Enter lengths in metres. Spray drop sizes are usually quoted in μm, so multiply by 10⁻⁶: 50 μm is 0.00005 m. Entering a millimetre value as is makes the result 1000 times too large.
  • Which density to use depends on the definition. For aerodynamic breakup of a drop moving through gas, the surrounding gas density is common; for the inertia of a liquid jet itself, the liquid density is used. Match the definition of the data you compare with.
  • σ depends on the pair of fluids and on temperature, and surfactants can lower it considerably.

Interpreting the size

When We is much larger than 1, inertia overwhelms surface tension; when it is much smaller, surface tension holds the interface shape. The critical value at which drops actually break up depends on how ρ and L are defined, on the surrounding flow and on viscosity, so this calculator does not decide breakup. One useful reference is the Ansys CFX theory guide. For We defined with the surrounding gas density, the slip velocity and the drop diameter, it classifies below 12 as vibrational breakup, 12 to 50 as bag breakup, 50 to 100 as bag-and-stamen, 100 to 350 as sheet stripping and above 350 as catastrophic breakup. If you entered a different definition, do not compare the result with these limits directly.

Examples you can follow with the calculator

Example 1. Recompute the fill-in example with tabulated properties

Given. The "Fill example values" button enters ρ = 1000 kg/m³, v = 2 m/s, L = 0.01 m and σ = 0.072 N/m. These are rounded inputs for a 10 mm stream of water leaving at 2 m/s; the speed and diameter are assumed conditions.

Calculation. With the button values, We = 1000 × 2² × 0.01 ÷ 0.072 ≈ 555.56. With tabulated 20 °C properties instead (IAEA density 998.2 kg/m³, OpenStax surface tension 0.0728 N/m), We = 998.2 × 4 × 0.01 ÷ 0.0728 ≈ 548.46. Halve the speed to 1 m/s and the button result drops to a quarter, about 138.89.

Interpretation. The rounded and tabulated inputs differ by about 1.3 %, the effect of rounding density and surface tension. Halving the speed, by contrast, cuts the result by 75 %. Speed errors are amplified most, so check the speed first if you have measurements. This value uses the liquid density and the jet diameter, so it is not one to compare with the drop-breakup regime table.

Example 2. Relative speed that puts a 50 μm drop into bag breakup

Given. A water drop 50 μm in diameter (assumed) sits in air at 20 °C and 1 atm. The air density is 1.2043 kg/m³, the IAEA Table 3.1 value of 1.166 kg/m³ at 98.1 kPa scaled to 1 atm, and the surface tension is 0.0728 N/m. Find the relative speed that reaches We = 12, the start of bag breakup in the Ansys CFX regime table.

Unit conversion. 50 μm = 50 × 10⁻⁶ m = 0.00005 m. The curvature pressure scale of this drop is σ/d = 0.0728 ÷ 0.00005 = 1456 Pa.

Calculation. Solving We = ρv²d/σ for v gives v = √(We·σ/(ρd)) = √(12 × 0.0728 ÷ (1.2043 × 0.00005)) ≈ 120.4 m/s. Entering ρ = 1.2043, v = 120.4, L = 0.00005 and σ = 0.0728 returns We ≈ 11.99; the gap to 12 comes from rounding the speed.

Interpretation. The 0.5 mm drop in the main guide reaches We = 12 at about 38.1 m/s. A drop one tenth the diameter needs √10 ≈ 3.16 times the speed. Fine spray drops are therefore hard to break with ordinary gas speeds, and this calculation works as a first screen for whether to switch on a breakup model.

Using the Weber number calculator

Can I enter the drop radius as L?

The calculation runs, but you get half the diameter-based value. Some references, such as the MIT surface-tension slides, define We with the radius, so check which length the data you compare with used and enter the same one.

My result is 1000 times larger or smaller than expected.

That is almost always a unit problem. A length entered in mm makes the result 1000 times too large; a surface tension entered as the mN/m number makes it 1000 times too small. Convert lengths to m and surface tension to N/m.

Can I use this calculator for a bubble rising through a liquid?

Yes. The surrounding liquid is what pushes on the bubble interface, so the usual inputs are the liquid density, the bubble diameter and the relative speed between bubble and liquid. Again, check that the correlation you compare with uses the same definition.

Does the result tell me whether a drop breaks up?

The calculator only returns We. If you used the surrounding gas density, the slip velocity and the diameter, you can compare with the Ansys CFX regime table in the interpretation section to judge the breakup mode, but viscosity and exposure time change the actual outcome.

Related calculators

  • Reynolds number (Re): With the same L and v, We/Re = μv/σ compares viscosity with surface tension.
  • Froude number (Fr): With the same L and v, We/Fr² = ρgL²/σ and speed drops out.
  • Strouhal number (St): The squared ratio of capillary time to transit time is We, so St is used alongside it when comparing oscillation periods.
