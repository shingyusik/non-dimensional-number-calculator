Weber number calculator (We)

Calculate the Weber number from density, speed, a length and surface tension. It compares inertia with surface tension where the shape of an interface matters, as in drops, bubbles and sprays.

A ratio of two pressure scales

We = ρv²L/σ divides the inertial pressure scale ρv² by the surface-tension pressure scale σ/L. The curvature pressure that makes the inside of a curved interface higher than the outside is 2σ/R for radius R; the surface-tension note computes 144 Pa for a water drop of radius 1 mm with σ = 0.072 N/m. A large We means the flow is strong enough to overcome pressures like this and deform the interface.

Points to watch when entering values

  • Speed is squared: with everything else fixed, doubling v quadruples We. Use the speed of the drop relative to the surrounding fluid.
  • Surface tension in mN/m (or dyn/cm) must be divided by 1000 to give N/m; 72 mN/m is 0.072 N/m.
  • Which density to use depends on the definition. For aerodynamic breakup of a drop moving through gas, the surrounding gas density is common; for the inertia of a liquid jet itself, the liquid density is used. Match the definition of the data you compare with.
  • σ depends on the pair of fluids and on temperature, and surfactants can lower it considerably.

Interpreting the size

When We is much larger than 1, inertia overwhelms surface tension; when it is much smaller, surface tension holds the interface shape. The critical value at which drops actually break up depends on how ρ and L are defined, on the surrounding flow and on viscosity, so this calculator does not decide breakup. Use it to compare with experiments or references that use the same definition.

Check with the example

The example uses ρ = 1000 kg/m³, v = 2 m/s, L = 0.01 m and σ = 0.072 N/m, giving We ≈ 555.56. Halve the speed to 1 m/s and We drops to a quarter, about 138.89. The surface-tension note calculates We ≈ 6.94 and Re = 1000 together for a 2 mm length at 0.5 m/s, then cross-checks them with We/Re = μv/σ.

Related calculators

  • Reynolds number (Re): With the same L and v, We/Re = μv/σ compares viscosity with surface tension.
  • Froude number (Fr): With the same L and v, We/Fr² = ρgL²/σ and speed drops out.
  • Strouhal number (St): The squared ratio of capillary time to transit time is We, so St is used alongside it when comparing oscillation periods.
