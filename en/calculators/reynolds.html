What Re compares

The Reynolds number compares inertial and viscous effects. Divide the inertial pressure scale ρv² by the viscous stress scale μv/L and you get ρvL/μ. With kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ, the same value is written Re = vL/ν.

Two geometrically similar flows of different size and speed have a similar balance of inertia and viscosity when their Re matches, which is why model tests try to match it. The converse does not hold: equal Re in different geometries can look very different.

Getting the units right

  • Convert a diameter in mm to metres by dividing by 1000; a 10 mm bore is 0.01.
  • Viscosity in centipoise (cP) converts with 1 cP = 0.001 Pa·s.
  • If your data give only kinematic viscosity ν in m²/s, enter μ = ρν. Putting ν in the μ field is off by a factor of the density.
  • For a circular pipe, use the mean velocity (flow rate divided by area). Using the centre-line maximum inflates Re.

How far do 2300 and 4000 hold?

The laminar, transitional and turbulent label under the result assumes fully developed flow inside a smooth circular pipe: below 2300 is shown as laminar, above 4000 as turbulent, and the band between as transitional.

These are guide values. With very low inlet disturbances, laminar flow can persist above 2300, while vibration or rough walls can trigger transition earlier. External flows over plates, cylinders and spheres use different length scales and transition at different Re, so ignore the label there. Non-circular ducts often use the hydraulic diameter 4A/P (area A, wetted perimeter P), but do not assume the circular-pipe thresholds carry over.

Try the example

“Fill example values” enters ρ = 1000 kg/m³, v = 0.2 m/s, L = 0.01 m and μ = 0.001 Pa·s, giving Re = 2000. Change only the speed to 0.4 m/s and you get 4000. Re is proportional to ρ, v and L and inversely proportional to μ, so predicting how the result should move when one input doubles is a quick way to spot unit mistakes.

What Re cannot tell you

Re characterises the flow but does not give pressure loss or heat transfer. Pressure loss also needs the pipe length and a friction factor; heat transfer needs the Prandtl number and a heat-transfer coefficient. The pressure-loss note shows how two pipes with the same Re can lose very different amounts of pressure.
