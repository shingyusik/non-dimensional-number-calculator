How Sc differs from Pr

Sc = μ/(ρD) = ν/D compares how fast momentum spreads with how fast the concentration of a species spreads. It is the Prandtl number with mass diffusivity D in place of thermal diffusivity α, which is why it is sometimes called the mass-transfer Prandtl number. Sc above 1 means the concentration boundary layer tends to be thinner than the velocity boundary layer.

Choosing a value of D

  • D is not a property of one substance; it belongs to a pair, a given species diffusing through a given fluid. Oxygen in water and a large protein in water have different D.
  • Molecular diffusivities are typically of order 10⁻⁹ m²/s in liquids and 10⁻⁵ m²/s in gases. A value many orders away from these is a cue to check units.
  • Data in cm²/s convert to m²/s by multiplying by 10⁻⁴.
  • Entering a turbulent (eddy) diffusivity as D no longer gives a Schmidt number for molecular diffusion.
  • μ and ρ belong to the carrier fluid (the solvent).

Reading the result

The example assumes a solute in water: μ = 0.001 Pa·s, ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and D = 1×10⁻⁹ m²/s, so Sc = 1000. Gas mixtures often have Sc around 1, while solutes in liquids commonly reach hundreds or more. In the same flow, heat can spread widely while a solute stays in a thin layer.

Oceanographers use temperature-dependent Schmidt numbers to estimate how fast gases cross the sea surface; the reference manual of the R package marelac describes this use.

Link to the Péclet number

With the same length and speed, the mass Péclet number is Re × Sc. The thermal and mass diffusion note uses v = 0.2 m/s and L = 0.05 m to get Re = 10000 and Sc = 1000, so the mass Péclet number is 10⁷. The same note shows that over 0.25 s the diffusion length √(Dt) is about 0.01581 mm, far shorter than the thermal length of 0.2236 mm. Entering D in the α field of the Péclet calculator gives the mass Péclet number directly.
